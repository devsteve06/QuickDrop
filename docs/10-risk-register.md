## How to read this register

**Scoring.** Likelihood and Impact are each H / M / L, scored 3 / 2 / 1. **Score = Likelihood x Impact**, maximum 9.

| Score | Band | Handling |
|---|---|---|
| 8–9 | **Critical** | Named owner, mitigation scheduled into a specific iteration, reviewed weekly, and gates the launch checklist ([11](11-launch-playbook.md) §1) |
| 6–7 | **High** | Named owner, mitigation scheduled, reviewed monthly |
| 3–5 | **Medium** | Named owner, monitored via a metric, mitigated if it moves |
| 1–2 | **Low** | Logged, no active mitigation |

**Owner legend.** The team is 2–4 engineers, so owners are roles, not people. Map them to names in iteration 0 and keep that map in this doc's companion issue.

| Code | Role | Primary surface |
|---|---|---|
| **F** | Founder / product owner | Market, legal, money, supply |
| **ENG-A** | Senior backend engineer | API, payments, ledger, dispatch |
| **ENG-B** | Full-stack / PWA engineer | Web surfaces, service worker, courier client, iOS constraints |
| **ENG-C** | Full-stack engineer (may be a 4th hire; if absent, ENG-B covers it) | Ops tooling, data, observability |
| **OPS** | Operations (dispatch and support) — can be part-time | Courier supply, support, incident comms |

**Status values.** `Open` · `Mitigating` · `Monitoring` · `Accepted` · `Closed` · `Realised` (it happened — convert to an incident record in [11](11-launch-playbook.md) §6).

## 2. Top 5 risks, deep dive

### R-01 — Marketplace cold start with no couriers

- **Likelihood:** High. Every two-sided marketplace fails this way at least once, and we have no courier density, no brand, and no track record to recruit with. We also chose the launch city, so we chose the exact geometry of the problem.
- **Impact:** High. Zero liquidity means the product has no value on launch day. The customers who arrive at an empty marketplace tell everyone. Recovering from a bad first impression in a single-city marketplace is slow, because the courier pool is drawn from the same city.
- **Concrete mitigation**
  1. **Recruit before announcing.** Target 60 couriers in a 3–5 km zone before any public booking channel opens. Recruitment is F's job, starting in iteration 1 — not at launch. Founder-led recruitment through local courier communities, campus groups, and existing gig-economy pools.
  2. **Demand first, in a controlled way.** Seed the zone with shipments delivered by founders and friends. These are real deliveries that generate real data and real tracking sessions, and they are the only way to find bugs in the app before strangers do.
  3. **Manual dispatch as a fallback mode.** A feature flag `dispatch_mode = manual` where ops assigns couriers from a queue view. If the dispatch algorithm is broken on launch day, orders still get delivered by a human. Cheap to build, and it is the difference between a bad day and a dead city.
  4. **Zone-level booking gates.** Take no bookings in a zone unless it has >= 8 available couriers and a 30-minute median time-to-assign over the last 24 h. Better to have one great zone than a thin city map.
  5. **Onboarding funnel measurement from day one.** Conversion from recruited to completed-KYC to first-delivery, tracked per cohort, because that funnel is the supply engine ([09](09-infrastructure-and-cost.md) R-03 coupling).
- **Owner:** F, with ENG-A on the measurement.
- **Iteration:** Recruitment starts in **iteration 1**; manual dispatch mode lands in **iteration 4**; zone gates land in **iteration 5**.
- **Residual risk after mitigation:** Medium. We cannot eliminate cold start, only shrink it and make it survivable. A launch day with 20 couriers instead of 60 still produces long waits. **Accepted with a narrowed blast radius:** one zone, capped bookings, manual dispatch as the floor.

### R-02 — iOS PWA background-location limits undermine the tracking promise

- **Likelihood:** High. The limitation is documented platform behaviour, not a bug we can fix: iOS gives no guarantee of frequent position updates to a backgrounded or closed PWA, has no Background Sync API, fires no `beforeinstallprompt`, terminates service workers aggressively, and may evict storage ([stack_report.md §2](../stack_report.md)).
- **Impact:** High. Live tracking is the product's differentiator and the reason a customer waits instead of ordering a competitor. On iOS — plausibly the majority of courier devices — the experience degrades to periodic updates or nothing, which produces "the map doesn't move" tickets and undermines the only claim that is hard for a competitor to copy.
- **Concrete mitigation**
  1. **Measure before promising.** Iteration 1 instrumented field trial with 5–10 real couriers on real iPhones, recording the distribution of observed update intervals: foreground, backgrounded, and app-switched. **The marketing and in-app promise is written from that data, not from the spec.** This is the cheapest, highest-value action available and it costs one iteration of partial effort.
  2. **Heartbeat plus explicit guidance.** Client sends a heartbeat every 30–60 s while on a live shipment. When the app is backgrounded on a live shipment, the client shows a persistent, non-dismissible "keep QuickDrop open to share your location" state. We are not asking politely; we are making it part of the job.
  3. **Offline queue without Background Sync.** Positions are batched into IndexedDB and flushed on reconnect or on `visibilitychange`, with an idempotency key per batch so a replay after eviction is harmless. No dependence on an API iOS does not have.
  4. **Server-side honesty.** On staleness past 60 s the ETA becomes `null` with `etaConfidence: 'unavailable'`, and the customer sees "we lost contact with the courier, we're on it" rather than a frozen countdown. A wrong number is worse than no number, and it is testable ([08](08-testing-strategy.md) B-12).
  5. **Installed-PWA measurement as a first-class metric.** `display-mode: standalone` versus a browser tab is reported per courier and is the strongest single predictor of tracking quality. If the installed share is low, the product promise needs revisiting, not the code.
  6. **Contingency, decided by data not by fear.** If the iteration-1 measurement shows a usable cadence in the foreground but nothing in the background, the choice is a **thin native wrapper via Capacitor** for the courier app only. It reuses our API, our ledger, and our dispatch — only the location and notification layer changes. Decide this by week 4 of iteration 1, not in week 12 of the build.
- **Owner:** ENG-B, with F owning the customer-facing promise wording.
- **Iteration:** Field measurement and heartbeat in **iteration 1**; stale-ETA behaviour and offline queue in **iteration 2**; `display-mode` analytics and installed-PWA nudge in **iteration 3**; native-wrapper decision gate at the **end of iteration 1**.
- **Residual risk after mitigation:** Medium for background tracking, low for foreground. We will deliver foreground-live tracking reliably and background-periodic at best. **The product promise must be written to match.** If the installed-PWA share is below ~50% at beta, we recommend a native courier app earlier than planned.

### R-03 — Courier KYC and payout onboarding lead time and failure rate

- **Likelihood:** High. It is an explicit open question in [stack_report.md §7](../stack_report.md), and every platform sees onboarding failures — document mismatches, name mismatches, bank-account verification rejections, and simply taking longer than a courier will tolerate.
- **Impact:** High. A courier who cannot get paid leaves, and a courier who waits a week to get paid tells everyone. Worse, this converts a solvable problem (recruit more couriers) into an unsolvable one (recruit more *cleared* couriers), which makes R-01 unfixable in the short run. It sets a **floor on time-to-launch** that engineering cannot compress.
- **Concrete mitigation**
  1. **Start in iteration 1, eight weeks before launch.** The first recruited courier begins KYC in iteration 1. The real lead time is a measured fact by iteration 2, not a launch-week surprise. This is the single most important non-code task in the whole plan.
  2. **Pre-fill everything.** Collect all KYC data at recruitment so the Stripe onboarding session is largely pre-populated and takes minutes, not days. Name matching to the bank account is the most common rejection cause, so validate it at recruitment rather than at onboarding.
  3. **Hard gate on live work.** No courier takes a live shipment until `charges_enabled` **and** `payouts_enabled` are both true. Enforced in the API, tested, not a convention.
  4. **Proactive chase.** Ops owns a queue of in-progress onboardings with a 24 h SLA to resolve anything stalled. A requirement flagged in Stripe's dashboard triggers a message that day, not three days later.
  5. **Plan for failure.** Assume a 20–30% onboarding failure rate and recruit accordingly — 60 recruits for a 60-courier target. Maintain a manual bank-transfer fallback for the first cohort, reconciled through ledger reversals rather than ad-hoc payments.
  6. **Document the flow once.** A single-page guide for couriers, written by F, tested with two real people who have never seen the platform.
- **Owner:** F, with ENG-A owning the Stripe integration and the `payouts_enabled` gate.
- **Iteration:** First KYC attempt in **iteration 1**; funnel measurement and pre-fill flow in **iteration 2**; hard gate and ops chase tooling in **iteration 3**; fallback payment path in **iteration 4**.
- **Residual risk after mitigation:** Medium. We will know the real number early enough to plan around it, which is the point. If measured lead time exceeds 7 days, we either narrow the courier cohort to people we can verify by another route, or delay public launch. **Kill switch: `dispatch_mode = manual` plus an invite-only courier list keeps the business running while onboarding is slow.**

### R-09 — Cost creep and negative contribution margin per delivery

- **Likelihood:** Medium. The pricing model is not the danger; the inputs are. AOV is an **assumption**, and anything that lowers it (promo pricing, a mix shift toward short trips, a bad fare table) raises the effective Stripe rate fast: 5.4% at $12, 6.2% at $9, 8.2% at $6.60 ([stripe.com/pricing](https://stripe.com/pricing)).
- **Impact:** High. Contribution margin of roughly $2.14 per delivery at a $12 AOV ([09](09-infrastructure-and-cost.md) §10.4) is not very forgiving. A few dollars of AOV, a few points of courier take rate, or a refund spike turns growth into losses at scale. Worse, it is a slow risk: it shows up in a monthly review, by which point the sunk marketing spend is gone.
- **Concrete mitigation**
  1. **Measure AOV and contribution per order in the seeded beta, before public launch.** This is the go/no-go gate in [11](11-launch-playbook.md) §3, not a later optimisation. If AOV does not hold, we change the fare structure before we spend on demand.
  2. **Contribution margin is a first-class dashboard tile with a floor alert**, visible to the whole team, not buried in finance.
  3. **Cap discount liability** so that a promo can never drive gross margin negative, and count discounts against contribution rather than against revenue.
  4. **Never discount to solve a supply problem.** Discounting with insufficient couriers makes both worse. Grow supply instead — this is the R-01 mitigation doing double duty.
  5. **Model-vs-actual drift review monthly.** More than 20% drift for two consecutive weeks triggers a re-derivation of the whole cost model ([09](09-infrastructure-and-cost.md) §10.6) before anyone optimises a hosting bill.
  6. **Cost per delivery decomposed into its variable parts**, so a margin problem is diagnosable: Stripe fee, courier payout, SMS, tiles, refunds, support cost.
- **Owner:** F, with ENG-C on the measurement and dashboard.
- **Iteration:** Fare structure and cost model in **iteration 2**; contribution-margin dashboard and alert in **iteration 3**; beta validation gate in the **launch** iteration.
- **Residual risk after mitigation:** Medium-low. Infrastructure cost is a rounding error against margin (roughly $160/month of operating cost at 5k orders), so the real risk is entirely commercial, which means it is measurable and therefore manageable. **The mitigation that matters most is validating AOV early.**

### R-17 — Money correctness failure in production

- **Likelihood:** Medium. The ledger is hand-rolled, per the pinned stack, and the failure modes — an unbalanced transaction, a double payout, a capture without a shipment — are exactly the kind that survive code review and surface under concurrency. "Unlikely" is not "never", and money bugs are found by auditors.
- **Impact:** High. Unwinding incorrect money movements is slow, public, and expensive; the ledger exists precisely so this does not happen. Trust damage compounds: a courier paid twice or a customer double-charged becomes a support escalation, a review, and a churn event. It can also create a legal and tax reporting problem.
- **Concrete mitigation**
  1. **The database enforces the invariant, not the application.** A deferred constraint trigger rejects an unbalanced transaction at commit time, so a bug in service code cannot corrupt the ledger ([08](08-testing-strategy.md) B-07). This is the single highest-leverage decision in the money design.
  2. **Append-only by trigger.** No `UPDATE`, no `DELETE` on ledger entries; corrections are reversing entries, always ([08](08-testing-strategy.md) B-08).
  3. **Idempotency key on every money write**, with a mandatory "called twice" test per path, and `Idempotency-Key` reuse with a different payload hash rejected.
  4. **A 100% coverage gate on ledger code that fails the build.** This is the only absolute coverage gate in the repository.
  5. **Nightly full-ledger balance sweep** across every transaction, and a **daily three-way reconciliation** against Stripe; any non-zero delta pages a human as SEV1. The same reconciliation query runs against synthetic fixtures in CI, so it is proven before it is needed.
  6. **Payouts derived from the ledger, never recomputed independently**, so there is exactly one source of the number that leaves the building.
  7. **Payout holds on dispute and on integrity flags**, so a suspected problem freezes money rather than sending it.
- **Owner:** ENG-A, with F reviewing any manual money correction and ENG-B as the second reviewer for every change to ledger code.
- **Iteration:** Ledger schema and triggers in **iteration 3**; charge flow with idempotency in **iteration 4**; payout batch and reconciliation job in **iteration 5**; ledger money scenarios in the **launch** iteration.
- **Residual risk after mitigation:** Low-medium. Database-enforced invariants plus exhaustive tests plus daily reconciliation make a silent corruption very unlikely, and none impossible. **Residual is accepted**, with the exposure bounded by the fact that any imbalance is detected within 24 hours by an automated check rather than by a customer.

## 4. Review process

1. **Monthly** while pre-implementation: F and the whole team walk the register. Update likelihood and impact with what we learned. Re-score every risk that had a mitigation scheduled in the iteration just finished.
2. **Weekly from the seeded beta**: every score of 8 or more is on the standup agenda until it drops. Early-warning triggers that fire become tickets immediately, not at the review.
3. **At each phase gate** ([11](11-launch-playbook.md) §3): a Critical risk with no mitigation is a no-go for the next phase. "We accept it" is a valid answer with a named owner and a written reason. Silence is not.
4. **On realisation**: status becomes `Realised`, an incident record is opened ([11](11-launch-playbook.md) §6), and the post-mortem asks whether the register predicted it. A risk that realises without warning means the early-warning trigger was wrong, not that the register failed.
5. **New risks** go in immediately, with an owner and a score, before they are discussed. A risk without an owner is a worry, not a risk.
